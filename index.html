Overview

In this project, I implemented de Casteljau's algorithm for evaluating Bézier curves and surfaces, along with area-weighted vertex normals, edge flips and splits, and Loop subdivision using a half-edge data structure, including boundary support as extra credits. I learned how simple operations can be composed to build complex geometry and how careful pointer management and automated invariant checks are essential for reliable mesh manipulation! I also discovered how mesh connectivity and triangulation affect Loop subdivision, as changing edge connectivity can improve symmetry without moving any vertices!

Section I: Bezier Curves and Surfaces

Part 1: Bezier curves with 1D de Casteljau subdivision

De Casteljau's algorithm evaluates a Bezier curve at a parameter \( t \in [0, 1] \) by repeated linear interpolation. Given \( n \) control points, one step lerps each adjacent pair to get \( n - 1 \) new points:

\[ p_i' = \text{lerp}(p_i, p_{i+1}, t) = (1 - t)\,p_i + t\,p_{i+1} \]

Repeating this step shrinks the point list by one each level. After \( n - 1 \) steps, a single point remains, and that point lies on the curve at \( t \).

I implemented one step in BezierCurve::evaluateStep(...). It loops over each adjacent pair \( (p_i, p_{i+1}) \), computes \( (1 - t)\,p_i + t\,p_{i+1} \) using the member t, and pushes the result into a new vector.

For the screenshots below, I made a degree-5 curve with 6 control points (bzc/curve3.bzc).

Part 2: Bezier surfaces with separable 1D de Casteljau

A Bezier surface of degree \( (n, m) \) is defined by a grid of control points \( P_{ij} \). De Casteljau extends to surfaces by running the 1D algorithm twice, once per parameter. First, each row \( P_{i0}, \dots, P_{im} \) is treated as a Bezier curve in \( u \) and evaluated to a single point \( P_i \). These row points then form a Bezier curve in \( v \), which is evaluated to the final point \( P(u, v) \) on the surface!

The three functions:

  • evaluateStep(points, t): one de Casteljau step, same as Part 1 but on 3D points with \( t \) passed in.
  • evaluate1D(points, t): calls evaluateStep until one point remains, then returns it.
  • evaluate(u, v): calls evaluate1D(row, u) on each row of controlPoints, then evaluate1D on those results with \( v \).

Section II: Triangle Meshes and Half-Edge Data Structure

Part 3: Area-weighted vertex normals

I walk around the vertex with its outgoing half-edges, starting at halfedge() and moving to the next one with h = h->twin()->next() until I return to the start. Each outgoing half-edge \( h \) belongs to one incident face, which I skip if it is a virtual boundary face.

For each real face, I take its three vertices in CCW order: the vertex itself \( v \), \( p_1 \) from h->next(), and \( p_2 \) from h->next()->next(). I then add their cross product to a running sum:

\[ n \mathrel{+}= (p_1 - v) \times (p_2 - v) \]

This cross product points along the outward face normal, since the vertices are in CCW order. Its magnitude is twice the triangle's area, so summing it directly weights each face normal by area. Finally, I return the sum normalized to unit length.

Part 4: Edge flip

An edge flip turns triangles \( (b, c, a) \) and \( (c, b, d) \), which share edge \( (b, c) \), into \( (a, d, c) \) and \( (d, a, b) \), which share edge \( (a, d) \).

Steps:

  1. Return immediately if the edge is on the boundary.
  2. Collect every element involved: the six half-edges of both triangles (\( h_0 \)–\( h_5 \)), the four vertices \( a, b, c, d \), the two faces, and the edge.
  3. Reassign all six half-edges with setNeighbors(next, twin, vertex, edge, face). The middle pair \( h_0, h_3 \) now runs between \( a \) and \( d \). The four outer half-edges keep their twins and edges, but get a new next, and possibly a new face.
  4. Reset the halfedge pointer of all four vertices, both faces, and the edge. This matters a lot for \( b \) and \( c \) since as they may have pointed to \( h_0 \) or \( h_3 \), which no longer leave them.

Following the spec's advice, I set every pointer of every element, even unchanged ones.

Part 5: Edge split

An edge split inserts a new vertex \( m \) at the midpoint of edge \( (b, c) \) and connects it to the opposite vertices \( a \) and \( d \). The two triangles \( (b, c, a) \) and \( (c, b, d) \) become four: \( (m, c, a) \), \( (a, b, m) \), \( (c, m, d) \), and \( (m, b, d) \).

I implemented it with roughly the same approach as the flip:

  1. Collect the existing six half-edges, four vertices, two faces, and the edge. If the edge is on the boundary, I make sure \( h_0 \) is its interior half-edge (see below).
  2. Create only what is needed: one vertex \( m \), three edges (\( m\text{-}b \), \( m\text{-}a \), \( m\text{-}d \)), two faces, and six half-edges. I set \( m \)'s position to \( (b + c) / 2 \).
  3. The original edge is reused as \( m\text{-}c \), and the old faces are reused for two of the four triangles. Then I reassign all 12 half-edges with setNeighbors.
  4. Reset the halfedge pointer of all five vertices, four edges, and four faces. As the initial comment says , \( m \)'s half-edge points along the original split edge.

Boundary edges (extra credit). If one side of the edge is the virtual boundary face, only the interior triangle \( (b, c, a) \) is split, into \( (m, c, a) \) and \( (a, b, m) \). The code for handling this is under the same interior case. On the boundary side:

  • The boundary half-edge \( c \to b \) is reused as \( c \to m \).
  • One new half-edge \( m \to b \) is inserted after it, so the boundary loop goes \( ... \to c \to m \to b \to ... \)
  • The old next of \( c \to b \) is already the following boundary half-edge, so the loop stays closed without traversing it.

A boundary split creates 1 vertex, 2 edges, 1 face, and 4 half-edges, and the new vertex lies on the boundary.

Part 6: Loop subdivision for mesh upsampling

All new positions in loop subdivision are computed on the original mesh first, and then the mesh is subdivided:

  1. Old vertices: for each vertex, I walk its neighbors to get the degree \( n \) and the neighbor sum. I store \( (1 - nu)\,v + u \sum v_i \) in Vertex::newPosition, with \( u = 3/16 \) if \( n = 3 \) and \( 3/(8n) \) otherwise. I also mark the vertex isNew = false.
  2. New vertices: for each edge \( (A, B) \) with opposite vertices \( C, D \), I store \( \frac{3}{8}(A + B) + \frac{1}{8}(C + D) \) in Edge::newPosition.
  3. Split: I split only the original edges. splitEdge appends new edges to the end of the list and never deletes the edge it splits, so I record the edge count first and advance exactly that many times. For each new vertex \( m \), I set isNew = true and copy the edge's newPosition into it. I then walk \( m \)'s edges: the two pointing to the original endpoints are halves of the old edge (isNew = false), and the others cut across old faces (isNew = true).
  4. Flip: I flip every edge that is new and that connects one old vertex with one new vertex.
  5. Update: Copy newPosition into position for every vertex.

Sharp corners and edges

Loop subdivision is "approximating" so every vertex moves toward a weighted average of its neighbors. As a result, sharp corners and edges get rounded off, and the mesh shrinks, so repeated subdivision turns the cube into a rounded blobby thing.

Pre-splitting edges near a feature helps with this. The extra vertices sit closer to the feature, so each average is taken over a smaller neighborhood, and so the feature stays sharper!

Cube asymmetry

Subdividing the cube several times gives a slightly lopsided shape. Each square face is split into two triangles by one diagonal, and the diagonals are not arranged symmetrically. As a result, the 8 corners have degrees varying degrees ranging from 3 to 6. Since the Loop weights depend on degree, symmetric corners are pulled by different amounts, and the error compounds each level.

Fix: split each of the 6 face diagonals before subdividing. Each face then gets a center vertex connected to its 4 corners, and every corner becomes degree 6. The connectivity now has the full symmetry of the cube, so each subdivision treats symmetric vertices identically. No more weird slanted squishifications!

Extra credit: meshes with boundary

Building on my boundary edge split from Part 5, I used the boundary rules from the Loop subdivision literature:

  • A new vertex on a boundary edge is placed at its midpoint, \( \frac{1}{2}(A + B) \).
  • An old boundary vertex moves to \( \frac{3}{4} v + \frac{1}{8}(v_1 + v_2) \), where \( v_1 \) and \( v_2 \) are its two neighbors along the boundary. They are found by traversing the vertex's edges and keeping those where Edge::isBoundary() is true.

These rules use only boundary vertices so the boundary subdivides as a smooth curve and is not pulled inward by the interior.
